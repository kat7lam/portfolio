The problem

Cannulation success is heavily limited by clinician experience and patient vessel characteristics, and in the field under time pressure the success rate drops further. ECMO uses an oxygenator and centrifugal pump to circulate blood and remove carbon dioxide during heart or lung failure. Without rapid blood-flow restoration, brain cells can begin dying within about a minute, and within a few minutes permanent brain damage becomes increasingly likely. Manual cannulation usually takes around 10 minutes; our device is intended to complete cannulation in under 3 minutes, reducing the probability of brain damage and expanding access to a capability offered by fewer than 5% of hospitals in the United States-and even fewer globally.

My contribution

Designed and iterated the electromechanical cannulation device in CAD and hardware, including stabilizing structures, a dilator clamp, and linear positioning for repeatable motion. I also built MATLAB-based ultrasound image processing to identify vessels by circularity, mark centroids, and guide positioning, linking mechanical design and image-based targeting into one integrated system.

Outcome

We demonstrated successful open cannulation of a sheep artery using our electromechanical device during an ex vivo trial. The design has been patented and is currently going through the industrial design process, translating the prototype toward a more deployable medical product. A paper on the work is in progress.
